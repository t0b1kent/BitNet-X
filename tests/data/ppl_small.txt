The water cycle describes how water moves between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land. Heat from the sun causes water at the surface of the sea to evaporate. The vapour rises, cools and condenses into small droplets that form clouds. When the droplets grow heavy enough they fall as rain or snow. Some of this water runs over the ground into streams and rivers, and some of it soaks into the soil, where it is taken up by plants or slowly flows underground. Eventually most of it returns to the ocean, and the cycle begins again.

A bridge has to carry its own weight as well as the weight of the traffic that crosses it. Engineers describe these two kinds of weight as the dead load and the live load. In a simple beam bridge the deck bends slightly under the load, so the top of the beam is squeezed while the bottom is stretched. An arch bridge works differently: the curved shape pushes the load outwards and downwards into the supports at each end. A suspension bridge hangs its deck from long steel cables, which pass over tall towers and are anchored firmly in the ground on both sides of the river.

Bread is made from flour, water, salt and yeast. When the ingredients are mixed, proteins in the flour join together to form gluten, an elastic network that gives the dough its strength. The yeast feeds on sugars in the flour and produces carbon dioxide gas. The gas is trapped by the gluten, and the dough slowly rises. After an hour or two the baker shapes the dough and leaves it to rise a second time. In a hot oven the gas expands quickly, the crust turns brown, and the inside of the loaf sets into a soft crumb.

The first railways were built to carry coal from mines to canals and harbours. Wagons were pulled by horses along wooden rails, and later along rails made of iron. At the beginning of the nineteenth century engineers began to experiment with steam locomotives. Early engines were slow and often broke down, but they improved rapidly. Within a few decades railway lines connected most large towns, and journeys that had taken several days by coach could be completed in a few hours. Cheap and reliable transport changed where people lived, what they ate and how they worked.

A computer program is a list of instructions that a machine can follow exactly. Programmers usually write these instructions in a high level language, which is easier for people to read than the numbers a processor understands. A tool called a compiler translates the program into machine code. Before a program is released it has to be tested. A good test checks one small piece of behaviour, states clearly what result is expected, and fails when the result is wrong. When a test fails, the programmer looks for the cause, corrects the mistake and runs all of the tests again to make sure that nothing else was broken by the change.

Honey bees live in large colonies that may contain tens of thousands of insects. Each colony has a single queen, whose main task is to lay eggs. Most of the other bees are workers. Young workers clean the hive and feed the larvae, while older workers fly out to collect nectar and pollen from flowers. When a worker finds a good source of food she returns to the hive and performs a dance that tells the other bees how far away the flowers are and in which direction they should fly. In winter the bees crowd together and shiver to keep the centre of the hive warm.

The moon takes about twenty seven days to travel once around the earth. Because the earth is also moving around the sun, the time from one full moon to the next is a little longer, about twenty nine and a half days. The moon does not produce light of its own; we see it because it reflects light from the sun. As the moon moves along its orbit we see different amounts of its sunlit side, and these changing shapes are called phases. The pull of the moon's gravity on the oceans is the main cause of the tides, which rise and fall twice each day on most coasts.

Paper was invented in China about two thousand years ago. Early paper was made from the bark of trees, old rags and fishing nets, which were soaked in water and beaten into a pulp. The pulp was spread on a flat screen so that the water could drain away, and the thin sheet that remained was pressed and dried in the sun. Knowledge of the process spread slowly along trade routes to central Asia, the Middle East and Europe. Today most paper is made from wood, and a large share of it is recycled and used again to make boxes, newspapers and new sheets of paper.

To solve a problem in arithmetic it often helps to break it into smaller steps. Suppose a shop sells pencils in boxes of twelve, and a school needs one hundred and eighty pencils. Dividing one hundred and eighty by twelve gives fifteen, so the school must buy fifteen boxes. If each box costs three dollars, the total cost is fifteen times three, which is forty five dollars. Checking the answer is simple: fifteen boxes of twelve pencils contain one hundred and eighty pencils, which is exactly the number that was needed.
